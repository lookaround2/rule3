INFORMATION NOTE
What this Part is about: Part 1 of the Alberta Rules of Court sets the
stage for all the other rules. It describes the philosophy underlying the
rules through purpose and intention statements, and then sets out
- the general authority of the Court to provide remedies,
-
the general authority of the Court to make procedural orders,
-
a means to correct contraventions and non-compliance with
the rules and irregularities in proceedings that are not fatal to
a claim,
-
the authority of judges to amend the rules, and
-
how the rules are to be interpreted.
Words and phrases used in this Part that have defined meanings in the
Appendix [Definitions] include: claim; commencement document; costs
award; Court; court clerk; enactment; file; judge; judgment; lawyer; order;
party; pleading; prescribed form; remedy; rules

PURPOSE AND INTENTION OF THESE RULES
What These Rules Do
1.1(1) These rules govern the practice and procedure in
(a) the Court of King’s Bench of Alberta, and
(b) the Court of Appeal of Alberta.¹
(2) These rules also govern all persons who come to the Court for resolution
of a claim, whether the person is a self-represented litigant or is represented by
a lawyer. [Alta. Reg.124/10;218/22]

These Rules do not apply to criminal matters (except for Rules expressly on criminal appeals
or prerogative remedies in criminal cases). But what is a criminal matter is not so clear any more,

purpose.⁴

Purpose and Intention of These Rules
1.2(1) The purpose of these rules is to provide a means by which claims can be
fairly and justly resolved in or by a court process in a timely and cost effective
way.
(2) In particular, these rules are intended to be used
(a) to identify the real issues in dispute,
(b) to facilitate the quickest means of resolving a claim at the least
expense,
(c) to encourage the parties to resolve the claim themselves, by
agreement, with or without assistance, as early in the process as
practicable,
(d) to oblige the parties to communicate honestly, openly and in a timely
way, and
(e) to provide an effective, efﬁcient and credible system of remedies
and sanctions to enforce these rules and orders and judgments.
(3) To achieve the purpose and intention of these rules the parties must, jointly
and individually during an action,
(a) identify or make an application to identify the real issues in dispute
and facilitate the quickest means of resolving the claim at the least
expense,
(b) periodically evaluate dispute resolution process alternatives to a full
trial, with or without assistance from the Court,
(c) refrain from ﬁling applications or taking proceedings that do not
further the purpose and intention of these rules, and
(d) when using publicly funded Court resources, use them effectively.
(4) The intention of these rules is that the Court, when exercising a discretion
to grant a remedy or impose a sanction, will grant or impose a remedy or sanction
proportional to the reason for granting or imposing it.

Related Provisions

questioning); 10.49 (sanctions) ; 10.52 (contempt)

B. Aims of the Rules
Proportionality of time and expense to what is in issue, is a very important principle now
in England,¹
and in Canada.²
Modern civil procedure has a culture shift to timely cost-effective
processes.³
Rule 1.2 gives added force to Rules on nonprosecution, such as R.4.33.⁴
Because
of proportionality, whether to have a jury was not decided, and postponed until other steps were
taken.⁵
Proportionality extends to R.1.2(4) and to suits over Minister of trafﬁc accidents, and trying
to claim obviously exorbitant damages.⁶
Rule 1.2 contains aims, and so is useful for interpreting the Rules, but subrules (1), (2) and (4)
are not directly operative and maybe should have been recitals, not Rules. They have confused
some judges or Masters and been used to contradict express Rules. Statements of purpose can
be looked at, so long as speciﬁc operational words in the Rules are not distorted, but statements
of purpose have less weight than other interpretive factors.⁷
If the conditions in a Rule of Court are present, that Rule can be used, there is no second
review of the question under R.1.2.⁸
The aims of the Rules of Court are to get to the merits as fast, efﬁciently, inexpensively,
and fairly as possible. The Rules are to be interpreted to promote those aims and access to
justice.⁹
Rule 1.2 subrules (2) (b) and (3) (e) call for the quickest route to resolving disputes and
so the courts must encourage settlement agreements, and the offers Rules about double costs
are important and beneﬁcial.¹⁰
At an early stage, getting to the merits and allowing amendments is
important if there is no limitation period or prejudice involved.¹¹
Or damages from a bad injunction
can be decided fairly summarily, without a separate suit.¹²
Since those aims often conﬂict, the
courts have to balance them; fairness is the most important aim.¹³
Making an objection to a minor
matter of form only after judgment is entered, violates R.1.2(3) (a).¹⁴

This Rule was used to prevent successive summary judgment applications by adjourning
an existing such application for more discovery, evidence, and written argument, in light of an
important issue pleaded but otherwise passed by.¹
Rule 1.2 was used to interpret R.10.49 (and 10.33?) broadly and award costs against a party
who disobeyed court instructions and made it impossible to prove notice or service to or on him.²
Rule 1.2 was not very helpful on whether a non-party could take part in an application to add him

It was used to weigh the seriousness of not clearly naming a defendant
once it is known.⁴
Rule 1.2 was used to permit a written application to dismiss a suit for delay under R.4.33, and
if that was denied, then in the alternative, to dismiss it summarily under R.7.3.⁵
Acrimonious litigation is contrary to the objectives of the Rules.⁶
The defendant is not required
to advise and assist the plaintiff, and litigation is still adversarial. Raising technical and procedural
issues does not contravene R.1.2, and there was no dishonesty by the Crown here. Nor do the
Rules trump speciﬁc requirements in the Class Proceedings Act, e. g. for a representative plaintiff.⁷
Extra costs were imposed on the losing plaintiff, whose very slow-moving lawsuit was just a
bargaining chip with huge exaggerated amounts claimed, intended merely to cost the defendant
money, and never intended by the plaintiff, who was an experienced litigation lawyer, to progress
to a result.⁸
C. Emergencies and Unusual Circumstances
Unusual circumstances such as an epidemic allow a King’s Bench judge to make small physical
changes to customary procedures, such as conducting examinations for discovery (questioning)
remotely by video hookups via computers.⁹
D. Limits on Using Rule 1.2
Of course R.1.2 is material to how to interpret the other Rules.¹⁰
But it cannot be used to
overrule clear language in another Rule, nor impose new duties on a party who did not have them
before.¹¹
Rule 1.2 is implemented by the “drop-dead” Rule 4.33.¹²

Some cases had suggested that the aims of the new Rules are novel and so far-reaching
that they overrule a good deal of the past procedural law, even where no speciﬁc relevant Rule
differed much from the old Rules.¹
But that approach has been largely overruled.²
The 2010 Rules
of Court did not wipe the slate clean and eliminate all the case law on the rules and procedure
before 2010. Such appellate decisions may be binding.³
The aims of the new Rules (in R.1.2) are
many, but not different from the aims of the old Rules, which were not intended to produce delay
or expense. Speciﬁc Rules (like R.7.1) cannot be bypassed by a chamber’s Judge own notions of
how to achieve the aims in R.1.2. Predictability has value in civil procedure.
Rules must be read in the light not merely of their words, but also their history, aims, evils
sought to be redressed, and the scheme adopted.⁴
Nor does R.1.2 let the courts invent new
powers or procedures.⁵
Even though the Rule on amendment of pleadings does not talk about
“determining the real question and issue between the parties”, that is one of the overall purposes
of the Rules under R.1.2(2).⁶
One case suggests that R.1.2 leads to a broader interpretation of the Rules, and so gives
more scope to the “analogy” Rule (R.1.7(2) ).⁷
But is that not backwards?
A Master thought that R.1.2(3) (a) on speed meant that summary judgment was possible if the
respondent ﬁled no opposing afﬁdavit. A judge reversed that because the respondent properly
got opposing evidence and raised considerable doubt, by cross-examining on the afﬁdavit given
to support the application.⁸

DIVISION 2
AUTHORITY OF THE COURT
General Authority of the Court to Provide Remedies
1.3(1) The Court may do either or both of the following:
(a) give any relief or remedy described or referred to in the Judicature
Act;
(b) give any relief or remedy described or referred to in or under these
rules or any enactment.
(2) A remedy may be granted by the Court whether or not it is claimed or
sought in an action.

A. Jurisdiction
1. Terminology
The word “jurisdiction” is used in several senses, and the principle limiting relief to what is
pleaded is not truly a question of jurisdiction, especially as the court can amend the pleadings,
and slip Rules apply.⁵
2. Source and Width of Jurisdiction
The Court of King’s Bench⁶
gets its jurisdiction from the King’s Bench Act and the Judicature
Act, which legislation gives that superior court all the powers of the Canadian predecessor courts
and of the English courts such as the pre-1875 Court of Queen’s Bench.⁷

3. Constitutional Limits
The Federal Court cannot get jurisdiction over topics outside Parliament’s constitutional
competence.¹
The Constitution is not a “law of Canada” for that purpose.²
And the federal law
must be essential to the disposition of the case.³
So it cannot hear a suit on a contract if Canadian
maritime law is not involved, even if an interest in a ship is involved.⁴
The provincial superior courts have inherent jurisdiction over constitutional questions.⁵
Provincial superior courts can give injunctions against the federal Crown in constitutional cases,
even outside the Charter.⁶
The validity of certain regulations (Federal child support guidelines)
can be determined by either the Federal Court or a provincial superior court. If the Federal Court
thinks that a provincial superior court would have more experience with this area of the law, it can
decline to exercise its jurisdiction to grant judicial review. The question was one of ultra vires, so
it was plain that the provincial superior courts could rule on the matter.⁷
4. Other Federal Tribunals
The Tax Court of Canada has exclusive jurisdiction to decide the amount of federal taxes, and
a provincial superior court cannot (even on an interpleader issue) decide any such question.⁸
But the Court of King’s Bench, and not the Tax Court of Canada, has jurisdiction over questions
arising incidentally, such as whether a company was revived.⁹
Where the background was taxes,
but the issue was damage allegedly caused by the government to the plaintiff, the Tax Court did
not have exclusive or maybe even any jurisdiction. And it certainly has no jurisdiction to decide
tort liability of the Crown.¹⁰
A suit in King’s Bench about Charter discrimination in income-tax
assessment may not necessarily be bad because not brought in the Tax Court, as it is based on
the constitution (the Charter).¹¹
The National Energy Board also has exclusive jurisdiction over certain questions,¹²
and so
does the Canada Industrial Relations Board.¹³
Sometimes a Band Council of an Indian band does
too, even if elected by custom, not under a statute.¹⁴

A. Equitable vs. Common-law Doctrines
The provision in the Judicature Act that substantive rules of equity prevail over common-law
rules does not apply to civil procedure.¹
B. How Firm are Procedural Rules?
The Rules of Court should advance substantive rights and not be a master of them?²
Any
Rule of Court is subject to an exception for abuse of process, and the court has a residual power
to prevent that.³
Often the courts will excuse breach of some Rule, especially if the other side
was not prejudiced (or the harm is being compensated) and the party seeking relief asked for it
promptly.⁴
A power in the courts to dispense with statutory procedural requirements does not exist?⁵
In
a province where the procedural law is truly codiﬁed (such as Québec), the courts must stick to
its framework and principles, and cannot create whole new procedures, but they can work out
the new implications (such as conﬁdentiality) of established procedures.⁶
Where a Rule sets out
speciﬁc tests or categories for a certain procedure, R.1.4 cannot be used to bypass them and use
that procedure in a category not named.⁷
Practice Notes are information and guidance, but do not
have the full force of law.⁸
Courts cannot make orders dispensing with requirements in statutes,
but they may do so with requirements in Practice Notes.⁹
Therefore a slight delay beyond the date
in the Practice Note was excused when it harmed no one.¹⁰

C. Inherent Jurisdiction
The idea of the court’s inherent jurisdiction is dangerous, and must be used sparingly,
especially when legislation covers the ﬁeld or has an inconsistent scheme or policy.⁶
Courts
should be chary of using inherent powers, especially when there is no functional gap or vacuum.⁷
Even the parens patriae power gives the courts no license to sidestep the Rules of Court; a Court
of Appeal has no jurisdiction to do so.⁸
The court’s inherent power does not extend to contradicting
express provisions of Rules of Court.⁹
Still less should the courts invoke supposed “inherent
jurisdiction” to bypass or contradict legislation, even to bar vexatious litigation.¹⁰
In a very urgent (life and death) case, small lapses in procedure do not invalidate the result,
even on appeal.¹¹
All courts, superior or not, statutory or not, have inherent power, ﬂowing from the common law,
to maintain respect for their authority, which includes power to manage and control proceedings
before them, and to control abuse in that regard, and to prevent misuse of procedure which is
unfair or which brings the administration of justice into disrepute.¹²

E. Stays
On stays, see R.1.4(2) (h), the Judicature Act, s.17, the Civil Enforcement Act, s.5(2), R.3.68
n. G, and R.14.1 (on stays pending appeal). The new Rule names no test for a stay of execution
pending appeal, but it is reasonable that the long-standing 3-part test from McCabe v. Westlock
R. C. Sch. Dist.¹
should apply.²
A second duplicative statement of claim is abuse of process, and not analogous to an amended
pleading (which is usually treated laxly). Especially when the new statement of claim is ﬁled much
later.³
Nor can the duplication be undone by a discontinuance of the ﬁrst statement of claim after
the court had ruled it improper. This is all waste of money and of court resources.
Two different lawsuits over the same transaction but against different defendants are not
necessarily an abuse of process, and even if they were, striking out is only one of a number of
possible remedies.⁴
Staying an individual lawsuit because someone else is bringing a class action whose class
would include that private suit, is often not a sufﬁcient reason to stay the ﬁrst private one. Like all
stays, one must use the usual 3-part test developed for interlocutory injunctions, and (among other
things) show speciﬁc risk of harm to the persons seeking the stay (here the persons proposing the
overlapping class action). Harm to the public in general is not enough. Individuals have a right to
sue alone for wrongs to them, and it takes compelling reasons to interfere with that right, and then
only to the extent necessary to protect competing rights.⁵
A stay over forum non is not a proceeding under R.3.68, but rather R.1.4(2) (h).⁶
Rule 1.4(2) (h) was used to defer discovery of records until after a motion to strike out or stay
the suit as against that defendant.⁷
Rule 1.4(2) is one of several reasons that the courts can issue
a judgment (such as a declaration) whose effect is postponed (for several months).⁸
Judgment may be temporarily stayed, to let the defendant enforce a collateral term of the
contract (that the proceeds are to be loaned back).⁹

One reason for a curative Rule is that nullity is often unpredictable and unfair. In a company
law context, it was said “Nullity is like a hand grenade in a conﬁned space. It sends a blast and
shrapnel in all directions, endangering the person pulling the pin and mere bystanders, as much
as the intended target. It cannot be aimed, and it can scarcely be lobbed. ”¹
Furthermore, nullity
can be used by a party as a “ploy” to avoid ever deciding on the merits, remaining silent about a
known defect until the limitation period to cure it has been deliberately allowed to expire.²
The Court of Appeal discusses when an interlocutory order can later be set aside or varied,
even if it is by consent. Consent orders differ, depending whether they are procedural or whether
they settle the lawsuit. Looking at the narrow grounds to upset a contract is wrong if there is no
contract or settlement of the substantive dispute. Consent may refer to a real contract, or just no
objection. If the order is just interlocutory and does not settle the substantive dispute, there is no
contract, and discretion governs whether later to alter it. If the delay in giving discovery caused no
particular prejudice, then usually time should be extended. Rule 1.5 supports this approach also.³

B. General Principles
Rules of Court are binding legislation, not mere suggestions.¹
The Rules of Court are like any other legislation and are to be interpreted by their language.
Other sources are to be resorted to only if the words in the Rule are ambiguous.²
If the wording
of a Rule or statute is precise and unequivocal, the meaning of its words plays a dominant role,
a well-established rule of interpretation. That includes the obvious implications of the immediate
context. Usual technical legal meanings of words are presumed to be used here.³
“Costs” has an
established meaning.
The Supreme Court of Canada holds that clearly expressed legislation (or Rules of Court)
about civil procedure must not be interpreted contrary to its wording, even to give effect to
principles or values of the Charter.⁴
A clear provision cannot be so neutered unless it is held
invalid on constitutional grounds after due notice.⁵
Rules of Court can supplement the Judicature
Act but not conﬂict with it. So if the interpretation of a Rule is unclear, the court should avoid
an interpretation which would make it conﬂict with a statute.⁶
The aim of legislation stated in
legislative debates is not to be achieved at all costs, and legislation can have multiple objectives.⁷
Like statutes, Rules of Court should be interpreted and examined as a whole.⁸
Re-enactment of certain words in a new version of legislation is presumed to carry with it the
same interpretation which the courts have earlier given to those words.⁹

This Rule preserves the rule that when an act had to be done on a holiday, it can be done on
the next business day. If the last day for service is a Sunday or a holiday, the time is extended by
one more day.¹
Similarly, if the deadline for appearing in court under a statutory time limit falls on
a day when the courthouse is closed, then the Interpretation Act says it is sufﬁcient to apply on
the next day that is not a holiday.²
In such cases, time is only extended to the next day which is not
a holiday when the ofﬁce is open; if the holiday or closure was for several days, one does not tack
all those days onto the end of the usual time period.³
