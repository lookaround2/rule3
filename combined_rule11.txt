PART 11: SERVICE OF DOCUMENTS

INFORMATION NOTE
What this Part is about: Many rules require documents to be served on
parties to a Court action. This Part describes how the documents that start
Court actions (commencement documents) and all other documents must
be served. Special rules describe how documents are to be served outside
Alberta. This Part also includes rules:
- for situations that require service to be validated, other methods of
service to be used (substitutional service), or service to be dispensed
with
-
describing how service of documents is proved and the time within
which a defendant must apply to set aside service of a commencement
document.
Words and phrases used in this Part that have defined meanings in the
Appendix [Definitions] include: claim; commencement document; Court;
court clerk; defendant; enactment; file; foreclosure action; judgment centre;
land; lawyer; order; partnership; party; personal representative; plaintiff;
property; recorded mail; rules; secured land; secured property; trustee

There is a discussion of the importance and purposes of service, in the Zahmol Prop. case.¹
The Rules are a code on service?²
Formerly the Clerk gave the original of certain documents to the party who procured their
issue. Most notable were statements of claim. The anomaly was that the ofﬁcial document was not
on the ofﬁcial ﬁle. Another problem was the danger of its loss. So any need to possess the original
document when serving a copy was dropped from the 2010 Rules.
An appeal from an applications judge requires ﬁling and service within 10 days of entry and
service of the order. That assumes that the winning party will prepare and enter and serve the
order being appealed. But where the applications judge directed the losing respondent to prepare
the order, that was done literally under the Rules but outside the expectations of the Rules. Service
is service; knowledge (because one prepared and entered the order) is not service; “self-service”
does not exist.³
If one cannot serve an endorsed copy of an originating application (or similar document),
because the Clerk of the Court has not sent one back within the ﬁling and service deadline, but
one has served an unendorsed copy within the time for ﬁling and service, it is enough to serve
an endorsed copy later once the Clerk supplies it, even if that endorsed service comes after the
deadline for serving.⁴

Rule 11.2 is not free standing, and does not create a new basis for service. Rule 11.5 does,

All litigants must maintain a current address for service on the King’s Bench ﬁle (R.13.13(2)
(f) ), at which they may be served (Rr.11.2(d), 11.15, and 12.56). If the litigant is also using an

Someone starting a lawsuit and naming an address for service
must appreciate that documents will be effectively served there, whether the plaintiff is there
or not.⁶
Too many self-represented litigants think that service is a one-way street, and demand
personal service. But a workable address for service is the price of taking part in a lawsuit and
getting notice.

A. What is Service?
Service gives the defendant three things: details of the claim or defence in the document, time
limits or times to respond, and warnings of the effect of not responding.¹
Service and notice are distinguished, and sometimes formal service matters.²
Service does
not need consent to be served.³
An individual is personally served by delivering a true copy of the document into the individual’s
hands or personally giving it to him or placing it in front of her.⁴
The individual need not consent,
and his or her rejection or attempt to return the document does not undo service. The defendant’s
picking up a document from his post ofﬁce box is personal service, and who hands the document
to him is irrelevant.⁵
Service is essentially a question of fact: did the person to be served actually get a copy?
So service by email might be effective, though unconventional, if admittedly the person opened
the email. It was risky, but it worked and was proved to have worked.⁶
Service by email of a
commencement document formerly was not a recognized method of service in Alberta, whether
or not it was to a lawyer of record. That the party declined to answer whether his lawyer had
actually received the email, was not sufﬁcient to show actual notice.⁷
But R.11.21 may now
change that and allow such service.
Does one have personal service only if the recipient knows the nature of the packet of papers
handed to him?⁸
Where the defendants noticed that there was a lawsuit pending against them and
knew the issues, and had requested documents from the plaintiff, that meant the defendants had
“actual and substantial though perhaps imperfect knowledge. . . ”. That was effective knowledge.
The object of service had been met. The defendants did nothing to rebut this evidence or its
inferences.⁹
Seeking to serve a person personally and explaining that is one’s purpose, but going
away still with the document because that defendant is absent, achieves nothing in itself.¹⁰

PART 11: SERVICE OF DOCUMENTS R.11.5(2)
Generally, service by recorded mail requires signature by the person to be served.¹
However,
if the document is also given to the defendant whose service is needed, by the opponent needing
to achieve service, that may amount to personal service, even though delivery was not intended
to be service.²
A defendant was sent an email asking if he was willing to receive the attached statement
of claim via email. She emailed back that “I conﬁrm that the document is received”. That was
good service of the statement of claim. Rule 11.18 allows written acceptance of service by a
self-represented party. Rule 11.30(1) (b) allows proof of service of a document in Alberta by
acknowledgment or acceptance in writing of service by the person served. There is no longer any
need today for a handwritten signature.³
B. Special Circumstances
Service by recorded mail at the last address for service given is valid and effective on
acknowledging receipt, or 7 days after sending, whichever comes ﬁrst.⁴
Recorded mail comes in two types: registered mail and express post.⁵
One type tracks delivery
and the other calls for the recipient to sign, and both give tracking numbers checkable online.⁶
Failure to pick up mail, refusing to accept mail, or absence when mail is delivered, do not invalidate
recorded mail sent to an address for service.⁷
A statement of claim is not served where a copy is provided with the express statement that
it is not being “served”, as service requires that the recipient be aware that legal rights are being
affected.⁸
The date of dispatch of a document intended to be served is not the same as the date of
service, and that assumption should particularly not be made when there is conﬂicting evidence
about the date of service.⁹
For example, Canada Post rarely delivers a letter the day that it is
mailed.¹⁰
If the cause of action arose in respect of a business, service may also be effected on a
defendant who is out of the jurisdiction but who carries on business in Alberta, by leaving the
document with a representative carrying on the defendant’s business in Alberta.¹¹
An individual being personally served no longer has the right to be shown the original, if a
proper copy of the document is served.¹²

Note the special Rules for service of personal representatives,¹
and litigation representatives.²
Sometimes the only really reliable service of originating process upon an individual may
be personal service, probably by an experienced process server.³
If the service is not upon a
person under 18 years of age or who lacks capacity, nor in a divorce action or action for nullity of
marriage, personal service may also be effected by sending the document by prepaid recorded
mail.⁴
But service of a statement of claim by mail or anything similar without an existing address
for service is very unreliable, because the person delivering will not insist upon the addressee’s
signing personally, and the addressee may well not be home when the letter is delivered.⁵
Even if

ﬁled is not impossible to obey, even if the court is conﬁrming ﬁling only long after the deadline to
serve has expired. Serving a copy without that ﬁling endorsement is not a nullity, and because of
the backlog the court had warned the public to serve unﬁled copies, and then later serve again
when ﬁled copies are received.⁷
Despite the notice, this litigant did not attempt to do that. The
legislation is quoted, and it just says to serve within the 30 days “the application”. It does not
mention a “ﬁled” copy.

2. Tests and Standard of Proof
The tests for jurisdiction to serve Alberta proceedings in another Canadian province, are
discussed in the Greenbuilt case.¹
Whether the plaintiff meets the tests to serve ex juris. is discretionary,²
and the court exercises
its discretion by balancing the interests of parties within and outside the jurisdiction. It is easier to
serve into another province than into a foreign country. Many cases discuss generally whether to
grant leave to serve out of the jurisdiction.³
The elements required for service ex juris. are a good arguable case on the merits, a real and
substantial connection to Alberta, and suitable forum.⁴
The test is fairly lax for the proof or strength
of the substantive lawsuit which is required.⁵
To serve out of the jurisdiction, for a cause of action,
the plaintiff need only show a prima facie case about the merits of the lawsuit.⁶
In service ex juris. , the onus is on the plaintiff to show a presumptive connecting factor.⁷
Injury
is necessary in tort, but it is not itself the breach, and place of the tort is not a simple black-and-
white test.⁸
A merely arguable defence is no bar to service ex juris.⁹
But if uncontradicted evidence shows
that the applicable law bars a suit against the relevant foreign defendant, service ex juris. is
impossible.¹⁰

C. Jurisdiction and Declining Jurisdiction
1. Jurisdiction Strictly Speaking
(a) Meaning of “Jurisdiction Simpliciter”
There is a difference between jurisdiction itself, and selecting a suitable forum.³
Suitable
forum (forum non conveniens) arises only if jurisdiction simpliciter exists.⁴
That has been less
discussed in Alberta where the tests are not statutory, and the tests for the two sound quite similar
for the two concepts. However the difference is very important. The two tests are actually very
different and are applied differently, and serve different aims.⁵
The phrase “jurisdiction simpliciter” refers to non-discretionary power of local courts to hear
a suit. In some provinces, statute governs that. At common law, it used to refer only to service of
the statement of claim within the jurisdiction. Now it refers to objective criteria to produce certainty
about real and substantial connection with this province (unless some statute says otherwise).⁶
Jurisdiction simpliciter is a minimum threshold, for the plaintiff to prove, though the defendant
can rebut the presumed jurisdiction.⁷
(b) Grounds for “Jurisdiction Simpliciter”
Jurisdiction simpliciter uses objective connecting factors and prioritizes order, stability, and
predictability.⁸
Jurisdiction simpliciter ordinarily does not refer to service ex juris.⁹
Jurisdiction simpliciter uses presumptive connecting factors, such as place of the tort.¹⁰
If they
are too weak, they could be rebutted at the jurisdiction simpliciter stage, but often that is better
done at the forum non stage.¹¹

2. Appropriate Forum
(a) Introduction
On attorning to the courts of one province, see R.11.31n. E.
As subpart C.1 shows, a court is not supposed to hear a suit if it has no real and substantial
connection with that province or territory. But even if the suit does have such a connection, the
court is also supposed to bow to a different forum which also has jurisdiction and is clearly more
convenient or appropriate.¹
In other words, the court here then has jurisdiction but declines to
exercise it.²
So the plaintiff has two possible jurisdictional hurdles. The aim of the appropriate
forum rules is to make the question of which court should hear the suit less rigid, and fairer and
more efﬁcient.³
The tests are described in subpart (b) below.
If true jurisdiction here in this province is found, the debate is not over. But then a defendant
wanting to litigate elsewhere, not here, has the onus of showing that elsewhere is appropriately
connected and is clearly a better place to litigate.⁴
Contrasted with jurisdiction simpliciter is this second forum non conveniens questions,
where a court with jurisdiction declines to hear the case. This second test emphasizes fairness
and efﬁciencies and uses a case-by-case approach.⁵

4. Procedure for Forum
Rule 11.25(a) requires that a commencement document “accompanied” by a document or
afﬁdavit setting out the grounds for the service outside Canada. “Accompanied” could mean ﬁled,
or served. Lack of accompaniment is a curable irregularity, especially if the defendants have had
the afﬁdavit in support for some time.²
A stay over forum non is not a proceeding under R.3.68, but rather R.1.4(2) (h).³
Any issues over jurisdiction, or better forum, should be raised at the beginning of the suit, not
the end, which is too late.⁴
Where forum non conveniens is raised, any questioning (examination for discovery) before
it is resolved should be conﬁned to issues of residence, convenience, place of harm, etc. , not the
merits of the suit.⁵
What is a suitable forum is a question of fact or discretion which is not lightly upset on appeal,
if the right tests were used.⁶
A decision on forum conveniens is discretionary, so appellate
deference to it is owed.⁷
But reversal is possible on many grounds, including unreasonableness.⁸
A motion to stay for inappropriate forum should be brought promptly, but need not be brought
before the statement of defence is ﬁled, and a statement of defence pleading the objection to
forum is not attornment to the court.⁹
A court usually cannot order a suit transferred to another province, so the usual remedy for
inappropriate forum is a stay of the inappropriate suit.¹⁰
But Saskatchewan has legislation allowing
a transfer in either direction, and s.8 of Alberta’s Judicature Act is enough to let an Alberta court
request or cooperate in that.¹¹

B. A Practical Method
People who evade service often hide behind family, roommates, and business associates.
Those people are usually too polite and hypocritical to do a thorough brazen job. They will pretend
that the defendant is away for a little while, or that the process server has just missed him. In such
cases, to swear an effective afﬁdavit to found an application for substituted service, the process
server should probably do four different things, and then swear to them:
(a) Watch the premises for a time, and show that the defendant lives or works there and is
evading service.
(b) Leave messages such as business cards (calling cards) for the defendant, to show openly
that the process server is trying to serve court papers, and leave a message asking the
defendant to phone or email the process server, and saying when the process server will
come by next.
(c) Show that the defendant was never home (or that no one would admit he was at home) at
the times when the process server had previously predicted that he would call, and that
the defendant never phoned or emailed the process server.
(d) Get the persons on the premises to conﬁrm that they give and gave all mail and messages
(including the process server’s past messages) promptly to the defendant, and will
continue to do so.
After substitutional service, especially on a tricky defendant, an enhanced afﬁdavit of service
proving its effectiveness, is very helpful.⁴

This Hague Convention sets what is the proper means of service abroad, and so actual notice
or receipt of the statement of claim probably does not validate the type of service.¹
The 2020 amendment to R.11.35 corrects a cross-reference, and shows that the procedure
invoked is the usual one for default judgments or noting in default.
