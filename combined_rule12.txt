PART 12: FAMILY LAW RULES

INFORMATION NOTE
What this Part is about: This Part applies to proceedings under the
Divorce Act, the Family Law Act, the Family Property Act, the Matrimonial
Property Act, the Protection Against Family Violence Act, the Extra-
provincial Enforcement of Custody Orders Act, and to family law matters
under the Change of Name Act and the Law of Property Act.
This Part also applies to restraining orders and to actions for unjust
enrichment in the family law context.
Unless a different procedure is specified in this Part, the other Parts of the
Rules also apply to family law matters. This Part is divided into Divisions
which correspond to the other Parts of the Rules, so that one can quickly
identify any exceptions which apply to family law matters.

A counsel appointed for children in a family law matter, is to give the court independent
information about the children. He or she is not just to parrot whatever the children say. A child

B. Geographical Jurisdiction
The court cannot hear a suit for divorce or a proceeding under family law unless it has
jurisdiction under conﬂict of laws rules; e. g. where the petitioner is domiciled. Mere convenience
or practicality is not enough.⁴
On geographical jurisdiction issues for matrimonial litigation, see
Halsbury.⁵
Alberta courts can receive a divorce suit only if either spouse has been ordinarily resident
in Alberta for at least a year on the date of its commencement. Corollary relief requires such
residence also.⁶

C. Recognizing a Foreign Marriage
Our courts will recognize a marriage performed abroad if it had formal validity (correct
procedure) at the place of the ceremony and had “essential validity” (capacity to marry) by the
law of the domicile. Public policy ordinarily bars recognizing a polygamous marriage, but if it is
a single marriage (no other spouses) and still is at the time the parties move to a monogamous
jurisdiction, it is transmuted into a monogamous marriage.⁸
Attornment by the defendant is also a
ground for recognition.⁹

A. Costs Generally

probably obsolete.

B. Security for Costs
Security for costs of a divorce proceeding was an important topic at one time, but is infrequently
litigated now. The court is not at all limited by R.4.22; quite the contrary.³
Family law cases are fully
subject to the Rules and principles about security for costs. A good deal of authority, and many
factors to consider, and what weight to give some of them, for ordering (or not ordering) security
for payment of spousal or child support, are set out.⁴
In a family or child custody case, the court
will not give an order for security for costs but specify in advance that not putting up the security
will not affect the rights or procedures open to the party told to put up the security.⁵
Where all
through the litigation, one party has shown that he is unlikely to pay any costs ordered, and it is
unlikely that any exigible assets of his can be found in Alberta, what total assets he may have
somewhere need not be determined. And if that party has never claimed that a costs award would
unduly prejudice his ability to litigate, and if the grounds for fearing error in the order under appeal
are not really explained and the order is entitled to appellate deference, those things are enough
to grant the respondent security for costs of the appeal.⁶
Where the proceedings which a spouse
is carrying on seem essentially a repetition of ones which he or she has lost before, he or she may
be ordered to put up security for costs.⁷

B. Interim vs. Final Corollary Relief
See also Rr.12.26n. E and 12.34n.
Interim relief is heavily litigated in divorce suits. That is for 3 reasons. First, because usually
the dispute is over the corollary relief, not over dissolving the marriage. Second, because it takes
time to litigate or negotiate a divorce judgment, and in the meantime the parties need to know
where they stand, e. g. who has custody of any children. One party may need maintenance. The
third reason is that many parties hotly contest the interim relief, and then compromise the ﬁnal
relief and do not go to trial. Before a divorce judgment, any order for corollary relief must be an
interim one only.
There are a number of appeals from interim corollary relief orders, but appeal courts discourage
these, and they waste time. It is often quicker to get on with discoveries and trial.³
There is a difference between interim and ﬁnal orders (on child support). Neither the Family
Law Act nor the Rules imply that all Family Law Act matters are to be decided summarily without
a trial, and they do contemplate interim pretrial orders. If facts and credibility are in dispute, often
a special chambers hearing will not sufﬁce for ﬁnality, despite its economy. Leave to give some live
evidence on the special chambers hearing does not mean that a later trial is denied.
Credibility cannot be decided on a paper record.⁴
An interim order on conﬂicting evidence may
be justiﬁed, if a proper hearing with live evidence soon after is already scheduled.⁵

conﬂicting afﬁdavits alone, one of which was by a child. Assessing the parents and the situation
at trial is usually necessary.⁶

A. What Circumstances Allow a Change
See also Rr.12.26n. E, and 12.34n.
At common law, judgments cannot be varied in substance, but the Divorce Act expressly
allows a change in the custody or maintenance earlier decreed in a divorce judgment, after there
has been a signiﬁcant later change in circumstances.
Under the Family Law Act, the trial judge can change a former Parenting Order without ﬁnding
any change of circumstances. That Act (like the Divorce Act) allows a variation order only if there
has been a change in the needs or circumstances of the child since the last parenting or variation
order.¹
But the Family Law Act and Divorce Act distinguish interim orders from orders. The Family
Law Act expressly allows a parenting order to be made for a deﬁnite or an indeﬁnite order or until
a speciﬁc event occurs. Cases on ﬁnal judgments of divorce or ﬁnal orders for custody under the

passage of time rarely if ever can satisfy that test.⁸

G. Appeal
The Court of Appeal reiterated its criticism of appeals from interim support orders, which
are temporary and often based on an imperfect record, and can be adjusted at trial. Time spent
at such an appeal would be better spent either settling, or having everything decided at trial.

It is unsuitable to appeal an
“interim without prejudice” parenting and support order, especially if a date has been set for its
review, even an appeal from special chambers. Instead there should be a quick trial. That has
not changed in the last 25 years. An appeal consumes too much money and emotion and time,
especially with a pandemic on. Such an appeal rarely resolves the underlying issues. Appeals are
not a “do-over”. And here (as often), some of the evidence was incomplete or lacking.⁷

PART 12: FAMILY LAW RULES R.12.61(1)
Appeal from the Court of Justice Order to Court of King’s Bench
12.61(1) To appeal an order of the Court of Justice to the Court of King’s Bench
pursuant to section 9 of the Court of Justice Family Law Procedure Regulation
(AR 149/2005), a party must
(a) ﬁle a notice of appeal in Form FL 33 with the Court of King’s Bench
court clerk at the judicial centre where the appeal is to be heard, and
(b) serve a copy of the ﬁled notice of appeal on the respondent in
accordance with rule 11.5 [Service on individuals] or as directed by
the Court of King’s Bench.
(2) The notice of appeal must be ﬁled and served
(a) within one month following the date on which the order being
appealed was pronounced, or
(b) if the appellant is able to establish to the Court’s satisfaction the
date on which the appellant received notice of the order, within one
month following that date.¹
[Alta. Reg.124/10;218/22;76/23]

Alberta Reg.207/2018 is the Court Rules and Forms Amendment Regulation, passed under
the Child Youth and Family etc. Act (effective Feb 28 2019). These Rules include the topic of
appeals under this Act from the Alberta Court of Justice to King’s Bench. There are many forms,
but the Rules themselves are brief. The Rules let the Alberta Court of Justice follow the Alberta
Rules of Court if the topic is not otherwise provided, but the Alberta Court of Justice need not
follow them. Breaches of the proper procedure are curable. For ﬂow charts and notices to the
profession about applications in family litigation, the “News & Announcements” part of the Court
of King’s Bench website, see R.12.26 n. A.
There is usually a right to state-funded counsel where the government is taking away children
from parents, but not where the proceeding is an appeal with no realistic chance of success on
the merits.²

12.71(1) No appeal lies to the Court of Appeal from a decision of the Court of
King’s Bench sitting as an appeal court for decisions made under the Family
Law Act except on a question of law or jurisdiction, or both, with permission of
a judge of the Court of Appeal.
(2) Part 14 [Appeals] applies in respect of an appeal under subrule (1).³
(3) Repealed by AR 41/2014 s2.
[Alta. Reg.124/10;41/14;85/16;218/22]
