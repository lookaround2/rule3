INFORMATION NOTE
What this Part is about: Rules in this Part facilitate Court actions by
and against personal representatives, trustees, partnerships, sole
proprietors, and other entities.
The rules in this Part also specify those individuals who must be
represented in Court by a litigation representative.
This Part describes when a lawyer becomes and ceases to be a lawyer of
record and the responsibilities associated with that designation.
Words and phrases used in this Part that have defined meanings in the
Appendix [Definitions] include: claim; client; commencement document;
costs award; Court; enactment; file; judgment; lawyer; order; partnership;
party; personal representative; pleading; prescribed form; property;
record; rules; trustee.

A great many cases outside Alberta have certiﬁed class actions, and a number of Alberta
cases do so also.¹
Evidence may not be as important in these certiﬁcation applications as it is in other applications
in the Court of King’s Bench. The strength of the cause of action is not really reviewed at this
stage, and the justice bases his or her conclusion on the statement of claim, not on evidence. And
most of the other statutory tests (described in Parts B to F below) are not factual topics amenable
to conventional proof by evidence and involve legal conclusions and the justice’s experience and
the submissions.²
B. The Test
Under this procedure, a few people can nominate themselves as plaintiffs and sue on behalf
of a class. Notice to all is necessary, and all will be bound.³
But other members of the class need
neither consent, nor nominate the named plaintiffs who sue. The tension (conﬂict) here is between
efﬁciency and fairness. Class actions are often economical and efﬁcient,⁴
share expenses, and
deter potential wrongdoers from breaking duties to the public. Their purpose is to give an efﬁcient
means of recovery where individual suits would be expensive or impractical.⁵
No court leave is required merely to commence a class action,⁶
though one can probably
count on a defendant’s challenging it once started. Usually the plaintiffs move to have the court
certify their action as a valid class action.
There are ﬁve necessary (but not sufﬁcient) criteria for a class action and its certiﬁcation:⁷

remedies, or subgroups with separate issues.⁶

by the delay and there was an arguable excuse, the delay need not bar the suit.⁷
Where a regulatory tribunal decided some issues for investors, but not all, and permitted
further suit, and some issues remained, and individual claims were small, a class action was
proper.⁸
What procedure is preferable is largely a question of law, so afﬁdavits and cross-examination
are not necessary.⁹

C. Lack of Standing
This is a separate topic, and is important beyond intervener status. See also R.3.69 n. B.
More important than intervention is whether a person has the right and power to sue, or to
defend a suit. But oddly no Rule expressly mentions this, certainly not in Chapter 2. (Rule 3.68

Not everyone has a right to sue about everything. Standing is about who can sue, and about
what.²
Lack of standing of a party is a ground to strike pleadings under subrule (1) or (2) (d) of R.3.68.

stake or genuine interest, and a reasonable way to litigate.³

intervener, even to make him or her pay costs.⁴

involved.⁶

and persons allowed to intervene or otherwise take part are not necessarily full parties.⁷

more interest in the matter does not cure it, and such attempts to bootstrap standing in litigation

The Court of Queen's Bench (now called King’s Bench) got a ﬂood of ill-founded habeas
corpus applications from serving federal prisoners, many of the applications drafted by one non-
lawyer inmate for his fellow inmates. Applications for two or more inmates were forbidden, and

the hopeless or vexatious applications.⁸
It is now generally followed in cases where a pleading
or court document has suspicious indications of abuse or odd ﬁctitious concepts. Typically the
court points out speciﬁc oddities or apparent deﬁciencies, and asks for written justiﬁcation or
corrections.
D. Procedure
Generally, there are no special Rules or procedures or rules of evidence for unrepresented
litigants.⁹
Self-represented litigants are not entitled to ask the court for a disproportionate share of
its resources, in order to remedy their lack of counsel.¹⁰
Though the Alberta Court of Justice Civil
Division encourages those without lawyers to come to court, nevertheless the trial process and
the rules accompanying are not to be waived or ignored.¹¹

Loyalty to clients includes no conﬂicts of interest, commitment to the client’s cause, and

The law ﬁrm (if it acted) would be both witness and advocate and counsel’s duty to the court is
paramount. The senior partner could act as his own “counsel” at trial, but not for a company of
which he is not the sole owner.⁴
Disqualifying counsel as a possible witness may be proper even if he may not testify. It would
require proof that he would not testify (and maybe an undertaking that he would not).⁵
On the
other hand, at an early stage in a suit, a mere possibility that a related lawyer will be called as a
trial witness is not enough.⁶
Someone who witnesses someone else signing a document, does not have to “validate” the
contents of the document, nor certify that the person owns the property. If signing is not in dispute,
having witnessed someone’s signature does not make a lawyer a likely trial witness thereby
disqualiﬁed from acting in later litigation.⁷
An afﬁdavit limited to formalities or uncontroverted
topics does not disqualify a lawyer.⁸
While a pre-trial application is still undecided, a lawyer giving evidence for the application,
and his ﬁrm, should not be counsel, nor ﬁle documents, nor appear in court, nor cross-examine
on afﬁdavits, though they can give their client (even the representative plaintiff in a class action)
legal advice. But once the application is ﬁnally decided, they can be trial counsel.⁹
But if the legal
assistant to one of the counsel swore an afﬁdavit, she is not necessarily counsel’s alter ego,
and counsel is not necessarily the de facto witness. Some of the afﬁdavit's contents were the
assistant's own personal knowledge (apparently from her familiarity with the ﬁle), and the other
parts are not clearly information from that counsel.¹⁰
E. Miscellaneous
A potential future conﬂict of interest (here a personal interest, not another client) is not enough
to disqualify, e. g. an allegation of professional negligence without any supporting evidence.¹¹
