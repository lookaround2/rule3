PART 7: RESOLVING CLAIMS WITHOUT A FULL TRIAL R.7.1(1)
DIVISION 1
RESOLVING PARTICULAR QUESTIONS OR ISSUES
[Alta. Reg.126/23]
Application to Resolve Particular Questions or Issues
7.1(1) On application, the Court may
(a) order a question or an issue to be heard or tried before, at or after a
trial for the purpose of
(i) disposing of all or part of a claim,
(ii) substantially shortening a trial, or
(iii) saving expense;
(b) in the order or in a subsequent order
(i) deﬁne the question or issue, or
(ii) in the case of a question of law, approve or modify the issue
agreed by the parties,¹
(c) stay any other application or proceeding until the question or issue
has been decided, or
(d) direct that different questions of fact in an action be tried by different
modes.²
(2) If the question is a question of law, the parties may agree
(a) on the question of law for the Court to decide,
(b) on the remedy resulting from the Court’s opinion on the question of
law, or
(c) on the facts or that the facts are not in issue.
(3) If the Court is satisﬁed that its determination of a question or issue
substantially disposes of a claim or makes the trial of the issue unnecessary,
it may
(a) strike out a claim or order a commencement document or pleading
to be amended,
(b) give judgment on all or part of a claim and make any order it considers
necessary,
(c) make a determination on a question of law, or
(d) make a ﬁnding of fact.³

B. Splitting A Suit
1. Introduction
This Rule lets the court split a lawsuit in two parts and try them separately. The biggest problem
with Rule 7.1 is that some lawyers (seeking shortcuts) try to use it where it is inappropriate. A
smaller problem is that in the occasional case where it is appropriate, the application is brought
in an unsuitable way.²
A factual dispute cannot be decided on a preliminary motion, and a mini-
trial on facts before the main trial is undesirable.³
Nor can an issue be intertwined with the facts.⁴
Rule 7.1(2) is conﬁned to questions of pure law, but applying a legal deﬁnition to a group of
settled facts is a question of law?⁵
An unusual order to try separately a single fact⁶
was upheld
in unusual circumstances of a questionable consolidation of two suits though one party had no
interest at all in big parts of the resulting suit.⁷
2. Splits Rarely Work
Bitter experience shows that this and similar Rules are usually seductive sirens whose song lures
a lawsuit onto the rocks.⁸
There is rarely any argument for a split other than the hope of saving time

3. Criteria
A case should not be split so as to yield two long trials.⁵
It might be proper to try a preliminary question of law as to the meaning of a word in an Act
and whether that would include a common-law relation. However, as soon as one suggests that
there is a constitutional issue and that there was a failure to include something, then facts become
relevant, and an order to try a preliminary issue is wrong.⁶
It should be clear and probable that time and money will be saved.⁷
Though a split is sometimes
justiﬁed, any saving of time and money is less likely where two matters proposed to be separated,
overlap. So for example, matrimonial property and support may overlap where the source of the
income for support is also one of the assets potentially to be divided as matrimonial property.⁸
The issue to be tried should be one which would end the suit completely if decided one way.⁹

C. Procedure
1. Consent
Rule 7.1(2) requires consent of all parties, and it hints that the parties should provide the court
with agreed facts or documents on which to base its decision of law.⁵
A judge should not order trial of a separate issue where no party has asked for that.⁶
2. Two Steps
There can be trial of a preliminary issue under R.7.1 only with prior leave.⁷
Rule 7.1 envisages
a 2-step procedure; a party cannot unilaterally force the court and opposing parties to hear, and
prepare the merits of, a split issue.⁸
See this book’s annotation of R.3.12 and the description of a “statement of the applicant’s
claim”. This issue to be tried is not a new action; that annotation describes the procedure where
pleadings are required within the existing action. Where a consent order deﬁned the issue and
directed which party would be the claimant, so the effective date of the “Statement of the Claim”
was some years earlier, the date on which the Originating Notice was ﬁled. The limitation period
stopped running then.⁹
Ordinarily the new trial ordered will be in the existing suit with no new
commencement fee, but not where there are no proper pleadings and the preliminary motion was
run out of some other motion.¹⁰

4. Effect of Order
When there has been a formal order that the court will consider issues in a lawsuit in certain
steps, a judge can strike out part of pleadings that are not relevant to that particular step.¹
Where summary judgment is granted except for one issue which is sent to trial, the judge who
tries that cannot raise another issue.²
He has no power to undo the summary judgment of the ﬁrst
judge, and litigation by instalments is a grave evil.³
Where an order is made for trial (assessment)
of certain losses, and the parties have not agreed to cover any other topic in that hearing, the
judge hearing that losses issue cannot dismiss the entire action, especially when other parts of
the action, if successful, might yield other losses outside the ones ordered to be tried.⁴
5. Bad Trial of Issue
If an issue is split and tried, but the key fact question in it is not decided, there must be a new
trial; and if the split was unwise, that trial will cover all issues.⁵
If the Court of King’s Bench tries
a preliminary question of law without citation of the leading case and without the evidence which
that case requires the court to weigh, on appeal the preliminary decision must be upset and the
preliminary question sent back to retry.⁶
Where a case is stated under R.7.1(2) without enough
facts to decide the question properly, the Court of Appeal may set aside any decision on the
stated case.⁷
On appeal to a judge from an Applications Judge’s decision of such an issue, if more
agreed facts are put before the judge, then that permits reversal?⁸
Summary judgment dismissing
a suit because of a limitation period, based on agreed facts, was upheld in the Castillo case.⁹
If there is truly no jurisdiction in the court to order something, then an order to try an issue as
to whether to order it, can later be attacked?¹⁰
6. Summary Judgment
Rule 7.1 does not allow direct summary judgment, though after an order under R.7.1, during
trial of the issue, summary judgment could then be sought.¹¹
The dissenting judge cited law, and
felt that a pleading calling for an order directing the opposite party to account, contemplated a
2-step procedure, so reaching a ﬁnal amount at trial was unfair; but the majority held that it was
fair, because the plaintiff gave enough warning to lead the evidence, at trial.¹²
Neither side got summary judgment in a defamation suit, though each proposed that the court
decide certain issues regarding certain parts of the impugned broadcast.¹³

once.²
If a preliminary issue is tried, the judge trying it can order that costs of it be paid forthwith.³
Where an issue before a tribunal is stated for the court to decide, the court can refer the costs of
those proceedings back to the Board hearing the main dispute.⁴
8. Miscellaneous
Ordering a joint trial of several issues (without pleadings) is not varying the Rules of Court.⁵
Discoveries can be postponed in one suit pending trial of issues in another suit, and a case
management judge is specially well ﬁtted to see when that is proper.⁶
Where the defendant denies
that he was a ﬁduciary, he must still disclose relevant documents for the whole period alleged, as
only the trial judge will be able to tell if and when he was a ﬁduciary.⁷
Where class litigation had
been bifurcated, in the ﬁrst stage afﬁdavits of records could be conﬁned to records relevant to the
issues on the ﬁrst stage only.⁸
In one case, preliminary issues were to be tried on condition that they not impede the progress
of a cross-suit.⁹
Someone raising a constitutional issue before a tribunal (with “Notice of Constitutional
Question”) cannot later complain if the tribunal does not decide that issue because of lack of
jurisdiction to hear constitutional questions.¹⁰
Rule 7.1 is about trial of one severed issue, not about striking out a statement of defence
entirely, and Rule 3.68 is the appropriate Rule for that.¹¹
9. Appeal
Whether to try a preliminary issue before trial under this Rule is partly discretionary and
probably reviewed by the Court of Appeal merely for reasonableness.¹²
The criteria found are still
those in the Esso v. Stearns Catalytic decision. It was reasonable to deny the split where the
case was almost ready for trial and facts were contested.¹³

(b) Exceptions to the Hearsay Rule
There is a little-known but useful judge-made exception to the hearsay rule. Someone who
has ﬁrsthand knowledge of a business’s system can swear to the system and show (for example)
what invoices and charges and payments it records, and swear that a search of those records
shows no other credits. That is not considered hearsay.¹
A large organization must be given a
way to give evidence.²
If the Canada Evidence Act applies, this is not conﬁned to accounting
records.³
Summary judgment does not need an afﬁdavit from every person personally involved in
the transaction sued for or involved in the preparation of the invoices. The manager of the relevant
accounting section can swear the afﬁdavit where a number of transactions are being billed.⁴
In a
historical case, expert evidence may be used of research e. g. into archives.⁵
But not all records
generated by a business are admissible.⁶
It is not enough to give tentative afﬁdavit evidence of analysis of records and vouchers with
no proof of expertise, nor even copies of documents referred to. Theft was admitted, but not any
amount.⁷
So an organization can have one person review its records and so get the necessary
personal knowledge, so long as the records are admissible evidence.⁸
That person need not be
the best-informed person.⁹
(c) Miscellaneous
Hearsay from a party’s own records is not admissible evidence of the truth of its contents, but
it is good evidence of what information and advice that party had.¹⁰
There is an extended discussion of the admissibility and weight of expert evidence, on a
summary (dismissal) motion.¹¹
No one is absolutely required to give its own evidence for a summary judgment application.
Rule 7.3(2) has two branches. It requires one of two things. The ﬁrst is an afﬁdavit, but the other
alternative branch is “other evidence to the effect that the grounds have been met”. That evidence
does not have to come from the applicant’s own deponent.¹²

6. Practicalities and Quality of Process
Now the biggest issue in most cases will be whether summary judgment will achieve a just
result.⁶
That word “just” sounds vague, but the Alberta Court of Appeal has given it more deﬁnite
tests. In addition to material or decisive fact disputes or credibility issues, there are other important
factors:
(a) source and continuity of evidence and other insufﬁciencies of the record;⁷
(b) a fair process;⁸
(c) proportionality, especially whether the lawsuit has huge consequences to one or
both parties or to the public;⁹
(d) whether the law is very complex or unsettled, thus requiring a full trial record.¹⁰
Speed, proportionality, and economy are important. Ordinarily they will weigh in favor of a
summary disposition, but are not alone decisive.¹¹
If there is a triable dispute but the expense of a full trial is not economical, summary judgment
often is not a proper solution, though some simpliﬁed process might be.¹²
Therefore, a party applying for summary judgment must look carefully at any evidence which
the other side gives in reply to the application. If it purports to deny or rebut at least one fact vital
for each cause of action, especially with details, then the application may be risky. If the plaintiff
is applying, he or she should also carefully evaluate the time which the application for summary
judgment would eat up, maybe instead get on with disclosure and trial (or settlement). Or seek
summary trial.¹³

3. Partial Summary Judgment
See also R.7.1 Part C.6.
If the afﬁdavit opposing judgment fails to answer one cause of action or one part of the claim,
the party applying should ask for judgment on that part alone, and a trial on the rest. If the afﬁdavit
opposing judgment for the plaintiff only questions the amount, the plaintiff should ask for judgment
declaring liability and ordering amount to be assessed at trial.²
Some older cases said that there cannot be summary judgment for part of a claim, but they are
wrong.³
Rule 7.3(3) has been drafted to make it clear that one can apply for summary judgment
on the part of the suit (liquidated or unliquidated) which is proven, and then carry on with the suit
to decide the rest.⁴
Sometimes partial judgment risks duplicative or inconsistent fact ﬁndings, but
partial judgment can signiﬁcantly advance access to justice and be the most proportionate, timely
and cost-effective approach.⁵
The fact that judgment is only partial is not a bar unless there is a
substantive reason to conclude that it would not be just.⁶
The Rules allow partial summary judgment, but the court needs a nuanced assessment to
see if in the circumstances that is appropriate. Partial judgment raises some risk of inconsistent
ﬁndings and duplicative proceedings, though it does often signiﬁcantly advance access to justice
and to a proportionate timely and cost-effective approach. Partial judgment also has inherent
expense and delay. The question ultimately is a just result.⁷
Other provinces’ Courts of Appeal have also suggested slightly different criteria for partial
summary judgment, though one would have to look carefully at the wording of their summary
judgment Rules.⁸

4. Procedural Bars to Summary Judgment
If there are issues which a party agrees to have tried, it cannot later get summary disposition
of them. Usually getting default judgment opened up bars a later application by the plaintiff for
summary judgment.¹
Though one should ordinarily attack one’s opponent’s pleadings only once,
not in instalments, there are exceptions.²
Would that apply to summary judgment also?
But a case management order directing the suit on to trial is only designed to keep it moving,
and is no bar to a summary judgment application by a defendant.³
Through error, a fact was
admitted in response to a Notice to Admit, and then on discovery of the error, a consent order was
made allowing the admission to be withdrawn, and that order recites that the factual topic “is a
genuine issue regarding a sufﬁciently important fact to warrant sending that disputed fact to trial”.
That recital does not bar a motion for summary judgment.⁴
The wording tracks the legal test for
withdrawing admissions, a minimum level necessary to withdraw the admission. Now that party
can argue that its position is even stronger on that issue, and that its position is right.
Since striking pleadings and summary dismissal are not the same, an earlier decision that
claims could be added by amendment because they could not be struck out, and were not
hopeless, did not bar later summary dismissal. The bar for getting pleadings amended is low. The
scope of summary dismissal is wider, more robust evidence is needed, and it can be sought at
any stage. It is not a collateral attack on the amendment decision, even if similar arguments were
used both times.⁵
If summary judgment is given on all but one issue, which is sent to trial, the judge trying that
issue cannot open up other issues and so in effect undo part of the summary judgment.⁶
But if
on a summary judgment application assessment of damages is referred to a referee, the court
retains power to decide the basis for assessing them and to clarify its earlier instructions.⁷
If the court denies summary disposition, if later the record is clariﬁed and the issues narrowed,
a second application for summary disposition may be possible.⁸
Though setting down for trial can be a bar to further applications, the court can allow such an
application even for partial summary judgment.⁹
The Jury Act does not automatically bar summary
judgment motions.¹⁰

PART 7: RESOLVING CLAIMS WITHOUT A FULL TRIAL R.7.4
Where the facts and law for summary judgment are clear, the court does not always have to
wait until the counterclaim is ready to try and then try both together. One cannot argue that it is
“inequitable” to give summary judgment now because one thinks the express contract signed
“draconian”.¹
Where there are two cross claims (e. g. main suit and counterclaim), after judgment on one, the
test for a stay of the other is like that for a stay pending trial or appeal.²
On a summary judgment motion, R.7.3 makes it clear that if the only real issue to decide is
the quantum of damages, the court can have a referee try that.³
