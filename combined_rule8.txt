DIVISION 1
MODE OF TRIAL
Mode of Trial
8.1(1) A court action may be tried by
(a) a jury trial under Division 1.1,
(b) a judge alone, or
(c) a judge alone at a streamlined trial under Division 5.
(2) Subject to Division 5, unless the Chief Justice directs that the mode of trial
shall be by jury, or in part by jury and in part by judge alone, the trial must be by
judge alone.¹

JURY TRIAL

Request for Jury Trial
8.2(1) An application for a jury trial under section 17 of the Jury Act must be
made
(a) by written request to the Chief Justice, and
(b) before an application is made to the court clerk or a judge to set a
trial date.
(2) The request for a jury trial must be accompanied with an afﬁdavit that
addresses the following issues to the extent that they are applicable:
(a) whether expert evidence will be called, and if so, how many experts
are expected to be called;
(b) the areas of knowledge or experience on which the experts will give
evidence;
(c) whether interpreters will be required;
(d) the number of court days required for the trial if a jury trial is ordered.

Information Note
Section 17 of the Jury Act sets out the right to a jury trial. It reads:
Right to jury in civil proceeding
17(1) Subject to subsections (1.1) and (2), on application by a party to the proceeding, the
following shall be tried by a jury:
(a) an action for defamation, false imprisonment, malicious prosecution, seduction or
breach of promise for marriage,
(b) an action founded on any tort or contract in which the amount claimed exceeds an
amount prescribed by regulation, or
(c) an action for the recovery of property the value of which exceeds an amount
prescribed by regulation.
(1.1) If, on an application made under subsection (1) or on a subsequent application, a judge
considers it appropriate, the judge may direct that the proceeding be tried by judge alone
pursuant to the streamlined trial procedure set out in the Alberta Rules of Court.
(2) If, on a motion for directions or on a subsequent application, it appears that the trial might
involve
(a) a prolonged examination of documents or accounts, or
(b) a scientific or long investigation,
that in the opinion of a judge cannot conveniently be made by a jury, the judge may,
notwithstanding that the proceeding has been directed to be tried by a jury, direct that the
proceeding be tried without a jury.
(3) In this section, “proceeding” includes a counterclaim.

C. Exceptions
Even the Jury Act bars a jury trial in a case with complicated scientiﬁc evidence or prolonged
investigation of documents.¹
The legislation uses the word “covenient”, but that refers to jurors’
ability to record, comprehend, and collate evidence, and to recall, assess and analyze the
information, not to disturbing the jurors or making them uncomfortable.²
Alberta courts often apply
that exception and deny a jury.³
But some cases are held not too complex, and a jury is allowed.⁴
That decision is discretionary and owed deference on appeal, especially if by a case management
judge.⁵
Another traditional exception to jury trial for equitable claims or defences is discussed.⁶

A case unsuitable for a jury need not be one with complex scientiﬁc or technical facts. Where
discretion is important for the decision, especially at more than one step, then a jury trial would
mean many issues would be taken away from the jury and decided by the trial judge, which would
require telling the jury to make sequential or alternative fact ﬁndings (not needed if the judge
decided all the issues with no jury). That would be impractical and inconvenient for all concerned,
and it would raise a high risk of unfair process and decision.²
There are a host of cases on when it is too late to seek a jury, and when a jury previously
ordered can be dispensed with.³
However, cases from outside Alberta may be inapplicable in
Alberta, as they seem to depend on express time limits not found in Alberta. One cannot object
for the ﬁrst time in the Supreme Court of Canada, that a jury trial was inappropriate.⁴

D. Summary Trial or Judgment
A motion for summary trial, and one for jury trial, are mutually and diametrically exclusive, so
the court should consider both questions together, and not take one ﬁrst, even on a preliminary
basis.⁷
How this Rule and the Jury Act interact with summary trial is discussed in R.7.5n. There
can be summary trial in a type of case where there is a right to a jury.⁸
Having a matter set for jury
trial does not mean that the Jury Act thereby bars a motion for summary judgment.⁹

R.8.3(1) PART 8: TRIAL
Deposit For Jury
8.3(1) When the Chief Justice grants an order directing that a trial be by jury, the
party who made the request must, unless otherwise ordered, within 10 days after
the date the order was granted, deposit with the court clerk a sum of money that
the court clerk considers sufﬁcient to pay the jury’s expenses, and if that sum is
later determined to be insufﬁcient, on notice from the court clerk, pay any further
sum that the court clerk determines in accordance with the Jury Act Regulation
(AR 68/83).
(2) Any money remaining after the court clerk pays the jury’s expenses must be
returned to the person who paid it.
(3) If the deposit for jury expenses is not paid, the trial must proceed without a
jury unless the Chief Justice otherwise orders.¹

A. The Policy
Form 37 is needed to set a matter down for trial. The Clerk has no power to enter for trial until
the form is ﬁled. Rule 8.4(2), (3) mandate what information is necessary. To conserve resources,
no case is to be set for trial when incomplete unless the Clerk is satisﬁed that things will be
complete in time. A signed exchanged form binds the parties before ﬁling.¹
Each side can rely on
the other’s representation and there are mutual undertakings not to take further pre-trial steps. So
such motions should be dismissed.²
Requiring a Form 37 requesting a trial date is much like the
former Certiﬁcate of Readiness.³
The heart of the matter is whether all pre-trial steps have been completed; from that question
ﬂow two common problems. The ﬁrst is a disagreement between the parties about whether
they have completed the necessary steps, e. g. , whether one party is moving too slowly with
questioning (examination for discovery), or wants too much of it.⁴
The second common problem
is an agreement by all the parties to set the matter down for trial and later to carry on more
questioning (examination for discovery) or interlocutory motions. That seems to happen a lot. If
that arrangement is well planned, and if the case is set down long before the scheduled trial, it
may be harmless.⁵

C. Conditions

D. Miscellaneous
The Court of Appeal may not expedite an appeal from refusal to adjourn a trial, especially as
little harm (except wasted expense) is likely to result from a trial.¹
The court accords considerable
deference on appeal to a decision by a trial judge whether or not to adjourn a trial.²
One can
complain on appeal about refusal of an adjournment only if one shows that withholding the
adjournment could reasonably be expected to have affected the result.³
It would be conduct
unbecoming for a lawyer (as a lay accused had done) to tell the Court that the other side could not
produce the necessary witnesses and oppose an adjournment, when he knew all along himself
where the witnesses were, and may even have told them that it was not going ahead that day.⁴

A defendant sued on a promissory note and guilty of some delay earlier, went to Africa shortly
before the trial, and had not prepared for the trial nor arranged for any of his witnesses. The court

merits; the adjournment was to allow to continue some attempts to compel more evidence. The
parties notiﬁed the court of the problems and cooperated, and were able to reduce the length of

B. Powers of Counsel
Counsel, including a prosecutor, has full discretion what witnesses to call or not, and the court
will not interfere unless counsel has an oblique motive.²
Counsel can call his or her witnesses in at
trial any order, but if the defendant or accused testiﬁes last, it may diminish the weight of his or her
evidence.³
The Court of Appeal cannot review counsel’s decision, barring rare cases of bad faith.⁴
Even a prosecutor has discretion whether or not to call certain witnesses. It is doubtful that
the court can ever compel a prosecutor to call a certain witness, especially if no oblique motive is
shown. Even if the witness would be helpful to the defence, it can call him itself.⁵
On a trial judge’s rare discretion to call a witness himself or herself, and then let both sides
cross-examine, see the Pasieka case.⁶
C. Procedure
The usual way to get any kind of evidence at trial is to issue a document which is called
either a notice to attend or a subpoena. It can be used where the witness is to give live evidence,
or merely to produce a record which he or she holds, or to do both. If records are involved,
it is sometimes called a subpoena duces tecum. Just as under R.5.13, this is not discovery
(disclosure), and actual existing records to be brought must be described in the notice (subpoena)
with some particularity.⁷

D. Objections to Attending
“Medical information” tendered to excuse a party from any kind of questioning before hearing

would alleviate any health risk. Some lay opinions were useless.²
One cannot sit silently on a subpoena for weeks and then just before the evidence is to be
heard, object that the witness has just gone away. Rule 1.5 allows the court to give an adjournment
to the witness, but on stiff terms, such as paying, reimbursing, or securing, revenue lost by the
delay.³
If a subpoena or the proposed evidence would be improper, the witness can turn up at trial
and object before the trial judge; or instead, the witness or the opposing lawyer in the suit can

5. Misconduct
During a long criminal trial, the Crown alleged that the accused was inducing or faking
medical emergencies to delay the trial. The judge shared the concerns, only after hearing all the
circumstances. That did not show bias, and the judge refused to recuse herself.¹
The Law Society can discipline counsel for improper behavior at trial, including lack of civility,
and there is a lengthy discussion of the criteria for that. But the courts can review such Law
Society discipline decisions on a standard of review of reasonableness. Civility must be balanced
against the counsel’s duty of resolute and fearless advocacy, especially from defence counsel in
a civil case. The limits of behavior are not simple, and ﬂow from context and facts. But alleging
misconduct by the prosecutor is permissible if it is sincere, though has no reasonable basis or is
incompetent.²
The trial judge is not to interfere unduly in counsel’s running of the trial.³
But in the right case,
the trial judge may tell counsel to tell his witness about the perjury sections of the Criminal Code.⁴
It is extremely unusual to hear ineffectiveness of counsel raised before the trial judge partway
through a long trial. That would require clear grounds and evidence that the incompetence (or
unethical conduct) of defence counsel could not be cured in any manner without a mistrial.
Counsel are presumed to be competent.⁵
A new trial was ordered where the judge told the Crown
to change its prosecutor partway through the trial.⁶
There is a discussion of what a trial judge should do and say if it appears that someone
(especially a party) is signalling to a witness; this is not the same as other types of observations of
demeanor.⁷
A trial judge should not signal to a witness how to answer or to get out of a dilemma,
during cross-examination.⁸
Letting a witness being examined speak to his counsel during an adjournment turned out to
be harmless, because the witness then only changed a bit the relief which he wanted, something
his counsel could have given (later) in argument.⁹
But such conversations are usually forbidden.
Counsel should be slow to accuse opposing counsel of misconduct, especially when it distracts
people from the substantive issues.¹⁰

C. Argument
Most of this commentary is about the law. For a full practical set of instructions on how to make
effective arguments, see Côté, Systematic Advocacy, Chap.19 (Juriliber 2017).
Generally on trial argument, see the Ferguson (Fuerst) book.³
It is fundamental that counsel be given a right to argue after the evidence is over and before
judgment is pronounced; lack of such a chance is fatal. That is violated if the judge sums up the
evidence and finds the facts contrary to one party, before any argument, and then asks that party
if she wants to comment.⁴
On the other hand, ignoring a party’s reply argument is not unfair, if the
reply is conﬁned to one issue, or materials, which the tribunal did not take into account.⁵
Though parties have the right to argue before verdict, often the judge will not call on a party to
argue a point on which he or she intends to ﬁnd for that party.
Who argues ﬁrst or last at trial is probably not so important if there is no jury. The party who
should go ﬁrst at the beginning of the trial is usually the one with the afﬁrmative burden of proof.⁶
There should be some limit on size of briefs. Even a complex multi-pronged argument can be
distilled, and not every additional citation adds materially.⁷
Questions or suggestions from a judge to counsel during argument are not judicial
pronouncements, and are often just exploration. They are not reasons, and are rarely evidence
of error or bias.⁸
A lawyer who misses deadlines for written argument may be criticized and lose the right of
reply.⁹

4. Important Legal Rules
It is important for counsel to know the law of cross-examination generally, but hard to ﬁnd that

without an interpreter.⁶

witness testiﬁed in chief.⁷

favorable to the cross-examiner’s client.⁸

5. Who Can Cross-Examine
Sec.26(2) of the Alberta Evidence Act allows one to cross-examine a witness whom one
called but proved adverse;¹
it does not let counsel cross-examine his own client, a party to the
suit, even if counsel is instructed by a liability insurer. A party cannot be “adverse” to herself.²
Counsel cannot lead evidence which generally discredits his own witness’ credibility, but he can
call two pieces or sources of evidence which contradict each other, and need not urge the judge
or jury to accept everything which his witness said.³
Who can cross-examine whom is set by who is adverse under the pleadings, so Mary Carter
agreements can cause complications.⁴
6. Miscellaneous
Unfair cross-examination is forbidden, and the witness is entitled to protest it or explain, and
the trial judge cannot rely upon the unfair parts, especially if the unfairness is pointed out in
argument.⁵
A trial judge must not interfere much with counsel’s trial cross-examination, unless there is
good legal ground to do so. Even with ground to interfere, the trial judge must not undermine
counsel’s function or strategy. The effect of interventions by the trial judge must be looked at
cumulatively, and in case of doubt as to effect, serious harm may be presumed.⁶
All that is doubly
so where the trial judge acts without objection from the opposing counsel.⁷
Trial fairness is a
distinct topic from bias by the trial judge.⁸
There is a discussion of order of cross-examination of different parties at trial. Evidence of
any party is effective for and against every party, so each co-accused can cross-examine any
other co-accused who testiﬁes. Usually the co-accused cross-examine (their co-accused or their
witnesses) ﬁrst, then the Crown does. Usually the order of names of accused on the indictment
governs which accused cross-examines ﬁrst. Ordinarily the trial judge should follow that, to
avoid wasting a lot of time arguing the order of cross-examination. Being last to cross-examine a
witness is commonly thought an advantage, but sometimes being ﬁrst is. But the trial judge has
a discretion in some cases to depart from the usual sequence. The sequence does not affect the
scope of cross-examination.⁹
If a non-party is required to undergo cross-examination, he or she is entitled to have his or her
own counsel present.¹⁰
The court may restrict re-cross-examination after re-examination of the witness by his counsel
succeeded, as it would largely repeat the previous cross-examination.¹¹
On what is “testimony”, see the Shaw case.¹²
Would that apply to cross-examination?

the criminal standard of proof never applies.⁹

D. Admissibility of Evidence
The ordinary rules of evidence apply as much on applications (motions) as at trial, unless a
speciﬁc statute or Rule of Court says otherwise; inadmissible evidence in an afﬁdavit should be
ignored.²
Evidence’s prejudicial effect should not outweigh its probative value. If it does outweigh, it may
be excluded. Waste of time, surprise, side issues, and confusion, are all part of prejudicial effect.³
In a civil case a judge should not admit all evidence and then adjust weaknesses by differing
weights.⁴
Opinion evidence cannot be entered outside of the formal regime for the admission of expert
opinion evidence under the Rules of Court.⁵
A party cannot simply hand in a letter informally on an application, with no advance notice
and no sworn evidence to identify or authenticate it, and thereby make it or its contents evidence.⁶
Materials in a legal brief, not sworn to by any afﬁdavit (nor proven by any recognized statute or
evidentiary rule), are not evidence. Nor should the book of authorities contain materials not in the
record nor veriﬁed by afﬁdavit.⁷
A chambers judge should not trench too much on a later trial judge’s power to rule on what
evidence is admissible, especially if that issue was not argued before the chambers judge. But if
the chambers judge does rule, the trial judge is not lightly to reopen such rulings, though they do
not absolutely bind at trial.⁸
A trial judge can revisit and modify pre-trial orders, and can thus admit
evidence (a witness) which a case management judge earlier ordered excluded unless he gave
discovery.⁹
A pretrial conference judge cannot bind the trial judge on admissibility of evidence.¹⁰

even for
handwriting comparison, and failure to object at trial is not fatal, especially if this objection is to

R.8.20 PART 8: TRIAL
After the plaintiff has led all his or her evidence, if the defendant wishes, he or she may ask
the trial judge to dismiss the suit (without asking the jury, if there is one). The non-suit motion is
decided at the end of the trial, not when it is made.¹
The defendant may do that because the law recognizes no cause of action on those facts,
or because the plaintiff has failed to give any evidence at all of one of the key elements of his or
her cause of action.²
The test for a nonsuit is to conduct a limited inquiry to see whether there is
some evidence for each element of the claim of the plaintiff. If there is, at that stage the judge
must assume the evidence to be true and give it the most favourable meaning where the evidence
could create competing inferences.³
Whether the competing inferences which the defendant might
rely on in this evidence would rebut the prima facie case, is not yet a question for the trial judge
to think of. That only arises at the end of a full trial.⁴
This is not the time to have the judge weigh
conﬂicting, weak, or improbable evidence, nor decide credibility.⁵
Virtually any evidence at all
of each of the key elements bars a nonsuit application.⁶
The judge can nonsuit because of a
reasonable inference drawn from the evidence, if the inference does not go too far, though he or
she need not draw all possible inferences.⁷
Any gap in the statement of claim can be ﬁlled by an
amendment at trial, if the amendment is otherwise proper.⁸
Where there are multiple parties, the
timing gets complex,⁹
and the tests stiffer.¹⁰

DIVISION 5
STREAMLINED TRIAL
Use of Streamlined Trial
8.25(1) The Court, on application by a party or on the Court’s own motion, may
order or direct that a court action be resolved by a streamlined trial if the Court
is satisﬁed that
(a) it is necessary for the purpose of the action to be fairly and justly
resolved, and
(b) it is proportionate to the importance and complexity of the issues,
the amounts involved and the resources that can reasonably be
allocated to resolving the dispute.
(2) An order or direction under subrule (1) may be made at any time, including
in chambers, at a case conference meeting or case management meeting, at an
application for judgment or at any other appearance.

PART 8: TRIAL R.8.26(1)
Application for Streamlined Trial
8.26(1) An application for a streamlined trial must be made
(a) by ﬁling the application in Form 36 and serving the application on
the other parties,
(b) by submitting a written request to the Court, accompanied with a
proposed consent order directing a streamlined trial, agreed to by all
parties,
(c) by making a request through the case management process, or
(d) as directed by the Court.
(2) At any case conference or on an application under this Division, the judge
may
(a) direct that the trial proceed, in whole or part, as a streamlined trial or
a trial.
(b) make a procedural order, including an order that the evidence of any
potential witness be taken before trial, or an order for security for
costs, and
(c) give any other direction to further the purpose and intention of the
rules set out in this Division and rule 1.2.
A judge should not merely dismiss an application summarily to ﬁnd a certain fact, even if
the judge concludes that the evidence before her is insufﬁcient, she should send the matter to a
suitable procedure in the Court of King’s Bench to determine the facts in question.¹
Dispute Over Mode of Trial
8.27(1) Any dispute about the mode of trial shall be resolved in a summary
manner, relying on
(a) the pleadings,
(b) statements by the parties of the issues to be resolved at the
streamlined trial,
(c) outlines of the evidence that would be called at the streamlined trial,
and
(d) other relevant information, if any.
(2) If the case conference judge or trial judge concludes that an objection to the
use of the streamlined trial was unjustiﬁed, the case conference judge or trial
judge, as the case may be, may do one or more of the following:
(a) make a procedural order;
(b) make a costs award for costs incurred as a result of the objection;
(c) impose a penalty under rule 10.49.
Note that ordinarily this lets the court decide “the mode of trial” without sworn evidence. Making
counsel swear to their plans for trial usually does more harm than good.

8-62
R.8.28 PART 8: TRIAL
Preparing Record
8.28 The parties have a joint responsibility to prepare the record for a streamlined
trial to ensure an efﬁcient adjudication, including by
(a) identifying the real issues in dispute,
(b) agreeing on relevant and material facts and records that are not in
dispute,
(c) ensuring that only the relevant and material evidence necessary to
resolve the dispute is contained in the trial record, and
(d) organizing the record and the evidence to expedite the streamlined
trial and assist the trial judge.
Scheduling of Streamlined Trials
8.29(1) Unless otherwise provided for in the streamlined trial order or a procedural
order, a streamlined trial shall be scheduled in accordance with Division 2 using
Form 37, with all necessary modiﬁcations.
(2) Where required by the streamlined trial order, a procedural order or a practice
note, the parties to a streamlined trial must participate in an alternative dispute
resolution process in accordance with rule 4.16.
(3) When estimating the trial time needed for a streamlined trial, the parties shall
include sufﬁcient preparation time prior to the commencement of the trial for the
trial judge to review the streamlined trial record.
(4) No later than three months prior to the streamlined trial, the parties must
schedule a pretrial conference or a conference under rule 4.10 to conﬁrm
readiness for trial and the amount of trial time required and to resolve any other
issue related to the management of the streamlined trial.
(5) The parties shall conﬁrm the scheduled streamlined trial date in accordance
with rule 8.7.
Procedure at Streamlined Trial
8.30(1) The rules in Divisions 3 and 4 apply to streamlined trials, with all necessary
modiﬁcations and subject to the streamlined trial order, any procedural order
made by the streamlined trial judge or any practice note.
(2) Subject to rule 13.18(3), the rules of evidence and any contrary direction,
evidence at a streamlined trial shall be entered by afﬁdavit.
(3) Unless the Chief Justice otherwise directs, if a streamlined trial is adjourned
after the scheduled commencement of the trial, the assigned trial judge shall
remain seized of the action.
Decision After Streamlined Trial
8.31(1) A streamlined trial is a full trial on the merits.
(2) The trial judge shall grant judgment after the conclusion of the streamlined
trial. [Alta. Reg.126/23]
